# Motion purpose: what motion may do, and what it may not

Richness is not the same as amount of motion. A video can be full of movement and explain nothing, or be nearly still and move at exactly the right moments. Read this before deciding to animate something, especially anything that is not clearly part of the explanation.

## 1. The governing rule

**The more attention an element is receiving, the less it should move for reasons other than the explanation.**

Whatever the viewer is reading, tracking, or trying to understand right now must be stable unless its movement *is* the thing being explained. Motion is permitted where it carries meaning, or where it sits in the periphery, steady and quiet, doing no competing work.

## 2. Three attention tiers

At any moment, every element on screen is in one of three tiers.

**Focal: the thing being explained right now.** Its motion should *be* the explanation (a signal travelling, a value sweeping, a failure happening). Everything else that competes with it should hold still. One focal motion at a time.

**Reading targets: text, numbers, equations, labels, captions, cards, tables, screenshots, clips being watched.** These must be still while the viewer reads or looks at them. They may enter (once, briefly), they may be highlighted by changing color, weight, an underline, or an outline, and they may leave. They may not drift, bob, float, breathe, sway, or slowly zoom while they are on screen to be read.

**Peripheral/ambient: connectors, backgrounds, secondary structure.** May move if the motion is steady, low-contrast, spatially away from reading targets and from the focal object, and means something (for example flow direction along a connector). It must never compete with the focal motion or sit under text.

Ask of every moving thing: which tier is it in, and does its motion fit that tier?

## 3. Roles motion can legitimately play

Motion is justified when it does at least one of these jobs.
- **Explain (demonstrate).** The mechanism itself: travel, cause then effect, a sweep, a pass over a graph, a failure being triggered. This is the main job.
- **Orient.** Show where something came from or went (a card slides in from the side it belongs to; a zoom into the region of interest, then holds).
- **Sequence (stage).** Reveal items in the order they should be read, one at a time, so attention is never split between two new things.
- **Signal.** Point at the important part (a highlight sweep, a circle drawn around the offender, a single brief pulse at the moment of an event).
- **Report state.** A change that reflects a changed quantity (a bar turns green when it fits, a status flips when the event crosses the line).
- **Keep continuity.** The same object moves to its new place so the viewer can follow that it is the same thing.
- **Indicate flow or liveness (ambient).** Dashes running along a connector to show direction, a node lighting in a pass through a flowchart. Acceptable when steady, peripheral, and consistent with the data direction.

If a motion does none of these, it is decoration. Cut it.

## 4. Motion that fails, and why

| Motion | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| A card or text block bobbing, floating, swaying, or "breathing" while it is being read | The viewer must read a moving target. Every direction change draws the eye. It adds nothing to the explanation. |
| Slow drift, pan, or zoom (a Ken Burns effect) on a card, screenshot, or clip card | The viewer cannot keep fixed positions in mind while the picture slides; details move out from under their gaze; it competes with the content. Fine for establishing shots in a story, wrong for information the viewer must read or inspect. |
| Looping pulse, glow, shimmer, or wiggle on a label or icon that stays on screen | Repeated attention-grabbing with nothing new to look at. Pulses mark an event once. |
| Parallax or moving background behind text | Moves the contrast the text is read against. |
| Per-letter or per-word animation on text meant to be read | Delays reading and fragments it. |
| Several things starting to move in the same moment | Splits attention; the viewer cannot tell what to look at. |
| Motion too fast or too complex to follow | The viewer cannot perceive what happened; it fails even if the idea is correct. |
| Motion that contradicts the concept (for example flow that runs opposite to the data direction) | The picture teaches something false. |

## 5. Decision tests to run on every moving thing

1. **Freeze test.** Pause the video at any frame. What is the viewer looking at? If it is moving and the movement does not carry the point, remove the movement.
2. **Removal test.** Delete the motion. Is any information lost (an explanation, an order, an orientation, a state)? If nothing is lost, it was decoration.
3. **Onset test.** Motion that starts draws the eye. Start motion only where you want the eye to go, at the moment you want it there.
4. **Reading-time test.** While a caption, card, or equation is on screen to be read, it and its container are still. Count words: about 3 per second.
5. **Congruence test.** Does the direction, speed, and extent of the motion correspond to the thing it represents (flow direction equals data direction; more time equals a longer span; a delay moves the thing later)?
6. **Budget test.** At every second, name the single focal motion. If you name two, stagger them. If you name none, the viewer is either reading (fine) or waiting for no reason (shorten).
7. **Camera test.** Zoom or pan only as a deliberate focus or reorientation move with a clear start and a clear end, then hold. Never a continuous drift.
8. **Ambient test.** If it is ambient: is it steady (no accelerations or reversals), low in contrast, away from text and from the focal object, and meaningful? If any answer is no, drop it.

## 6. Element by element: what may move

| Element | May do | May not |
|---|---|---|
| Info card, text block, equation, label, table | Enter once (short fade or rise, never more than about half a second); change color, weight or underline to emphasize; exit | Bob, float, sway, drift, slow zoom, pulse on loop, wiggle while on screen |
| Screenshot or image card | Enter once; a one-time purposeful push-in or crop-pan to the region the caption refers to, then hold still; a highlight rectangle drawn on it | Continuous slow zoom or pan; floating; tilt animations |
| Video clip card | The clip's own playback; enter and exit | Extra drift, zoom, bob layered on top of footage that is already moving |
| Connector or arrow | Draw on; a marker travels along it; steady dashes flowing in the data direction (ambient) | Wobbling, pulsing in place, flowing against the data direction |
| Node in a flowchart or graph | Lights up in sequence as a pass reaches it; a state change; a one-time pulse at an event | Idle bobbing, continuous glow |
| Bar, gauge, fill | Fills to its value; changes with the driver; changes color at the threshold | Idle shimmer |
| Number | A brief count to the new value if the change matters; recolor when it changes meaning | Flicker; counting for decoration |
| Background | Static, or an imperceptible, slow, non-semantic gradient far from content | Parallax or movement that crosses content |
| Highlight or marker | Move to the next item being discussed; draw once | Loop forever |

## 7. Ambient motion: when it is fine

Ambient motion is acceptable, and often good, when all of these hold:
- It runs in the periphery of what the viewer is attending to, never under text or a card being read.
- It is steady: constant speed, no reversals or sudden accelerations (each reversal is a new onset that draws the eye).
- It is low in contrast compared with the focal object.
- It means something or at least asserts something true (flow direction equals data direction; "this link is live").
- It does not run alongside a long stretch of reading without a break. Long parallel movement is a known distraction and a barrier for many viewers; for a video, treat any loop beside text that stays on screen more than about 5 seconds as suspect.
- Removing it would not hurt, and keeping it adds a little clarity or life. If it is the *only* motion on screen for a long time, the scene is a slide with a screensaver.

Examples that pass: dashes running along a data connector in its flow direction; a node-by-node highlight sweeping through a flowchart; a faint marker path being traced. Examples that fail: cards bobbing; a clip card slowly zooming; a glowing icon on loop; breathing text.

## 8. Evidence behind the rules

These guidelines come from research on instructional and screen animation and from accessibility standards. The research findings are paraphrased from sources checked while writing this skill; the application to specific effects in the last column is inference and is marked.

- **Animation does not help just by existing.** Tversky, Morrison and Betrancourt (2002, *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*) reviewed studies of animated versus static graphics. Apparent advantages of animation often came from extra information or interactivity rather than from motion itself. They propose two principles: the **Congruence Principle** (the structure and content of the graphic should correspond to the structure and content of what is conveyed) and the **Apprehension Principle** (the graphic must be accurately perceived and appropriately conceived; animations are often too complex or too fast to be accurately perceived). They find animation more promising for things like real-time reorientation in time and space. *Application:* motion should map to a real change over time in the content (congruence), and be slow and simple enough to follow (apprehension).
- **Extraneous material hurts learning.** Mayer's multimedia-learning principles: the **coherence principle** (people learn better when extraneous material is excluded; the engaging-but-irrelevant extras are called seductive details), the **signaling principle** (people learn better when cues highlight the organization of the essential material), the **temporal contiguity principle** (corresponding animation and narration should happen together), and the **segmenting principle** (learner-paced segments beat continuous presentation). *Application:* idle motion on information cards is extraneous material; highlighting and ordered reveals are signaling; captions should arrive with the motion they describe; leave pauses for the viewer.
- **Motion captures attention, and starts of motion especially.** Abrams and Christ (2003, *Psychological Science*) found that the onset of motion captures attention while ongoing motion is relatively ineffective at it. Franconeri and Simons argued that some kinds of motion capture attention even without an onset, and the exact account is still debated. *Application (inference):* a sine bob re-triggers changes in motion on every swing, so it keeps asking for attention; steady uniform motion in the periphery is the safest kind of ambient motion.
- **Moving content next to other content is a known barrier.** WCAG 2.2.2 (Pause, Stop, Hide) requires a way to pause, stop, or hide any moving content that starts automatically, lasts more than five seconds, and is presented in parallel with other content. The W3C explains that moving content can be a barrier for people who have trouble reading stationary text quickly or tracking moving objects, and can be a severe distraction, particularly for people with attention disorders. *Application:* a video cannot rely on the viewer being able to switch off a loop, so keep long loops away from reading targets.
- **Staging.** A classic principle of traditional animation: direct the audience's attention to what matters and do not let competing actions steal it. *Application:* one focal motion at a time.

Effect sizes vary across studies, and most evidence comes from instructional material and interfaces rather than explainer videos in particular. Treat these as strong defaults, not laws. If the user explicitly wants a stylized or decorative treatment, follow the user.

## 9. Review checklist

For every scene, list each moving thing and answer:
- Which tier is it (focal, reading target, ambient)?
- If it is a reading target, is it still while on screen? (Only entering and leaving motion allowed.)
- If it is focal, is the motion the explanation (congruent with the content, slow and simple enough to follow)?
- If it is ambient, does it pass the ambient test in section 7?
- Does any card, screenshot, or clip card have continuous motion (drift, zoom, bob)? Remove it.
- At every second, is there one clear focal motion, or is the viewer deliberately given time to read?
- Review the rendered slide at each teaching click. Check sustained motion while reading and after leaving the slide. Spot-check reduced motion and print when the deck targets those modes. See the [final browser checklist](quarto-and-qa.md#final-browser-checklist).
